## First steps after a house fire

1. **Wait for the all-clear.** The American Red Cross says to stay out of a fire-damaged home until local fire authorities say it's safe to go back in.
2. **Call your insurance company.** The U.S. Fire Administration advises contacting your insurer right away and asking what to do first.
3. **Check for hazards.** Look for sagging floors and ceilings. Don't turn on power or gas until utility workers or an electrician have checked them.
4. **Secure the home.** Broken windows and roof holes need board-up or tarps to keep out rain and intruders.
5. **Take photos and video** of every room before cleaning.
6. **Throw out exposed food and medicine.** The Red Cross says to discard food, drinks and medicine exposed to heat, smoke or soot.

## Why smoke damage is hard to clean

Smoke is made of tiny particles and gases that settle on every surface and get pulled into cracks, fabrics and insulation. The U.S. Fire Administration notes that the soot and dirty water left behind may contain things that could make you sick. Soot is also oily. The Red Cross warns that it smears easily and leaves black stains on whatever it touches.

| Residue type | Common source | What it's like |
|---|---|---|
| Dry smoke | Fast, hot fires with wood or paper | Powdery, easier to vacuum |
| Wet smoke | Slow, smoldering fires with plastic or rubber | Sticky, smeary, strong odor |
| Protein residue | Kitchen fires that burn food | Thin yellowish film, very strong odor |
| Fuel oil soot | Furnace puff-backs | Dark, oily film throughout the home |

## How to clean light smoke damage yourself

For small amounts of residue on hard surfaces, the American Red Cross gives these steps:

!checks
- **Don't wipe soot first.** Vacuum loose soot with a shop vac, keeping the nozzle from touching the surface.
- **Ventilate.** Open windows and run fans to move smoky air out, when the weather allows.
- **Wash hard surfaces** with a mild soap or detergent. The Red Cross also describes a mix of 4 to 6 tablespoons of trisodium phosphate (TSP) and 1 cup of household cleaner or chlorine bleach per gallon of warm water. Wear rubber gloves and follow the label.
- **Work in small sections.** Wash walls from the floor up to prevent streaks, rinse right away, and do ceilings last.
- **Let everything dry** fully before you repaint.
- **Launder washable fabrics.** Test first. More than one wash may be needed.

Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. Wear gloves, eye protection and old clothes.

## What usually has to be thrown away

!checks
- Food, drinks and medicine exposed to heat, smoke or soot
- Mattresses and pillows with heavy smoke odor
- Burned or melted plastics
- Drywall and insulation soaked by fire hoses
- Stuffed toys and porous items that can't be cleaned
- Cosmetics and hygiene items near the fire

The Red Cross notes that drywall and insulation soaked by fire hoses can't be dried out and keep their strength or their resistance to mold, so talk with a professional about replacing them.

## The water damage behind the smoke damage

Many homeowners focus on soot and forget the water. Firefighters may use a lot of it, and the U.S. Fire Administration notes they may also break windows or cut holes in walls and the roof to fight the fire. That water runs into wall cavities, under flooring and into rooms below. The EPA says wet materials need drying within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. That's why drying equipment should go in early, even while the smoke cleanup is being planned. ## How professionals remove smoke odor

Smoke smell comes from residue, so removing odor means removing the residue first. A professional crew will:

1. **Clean or remove** every surface with residue, including inside cabinets and closets.
2. **Use the right cleaner** for each residue type, such as dry chemical sponges for dry soot.
3. **Remove odor sources** that can't be cleaned, such as smoky insulation. The Red Cross notes insulation often holds odor.
4. **Run HEPA air scrubbers** to capture fine particles.
5. **Treat remaining odor** with methods that reach hidden spaces, such as thermal fogging or hydroxyl units.
6. **Clean the HVAC system** so it doesn't spread odor again.

Air fresheners and candles only cover the smell for a short time.

## Insurance and smoke damage in Maryland

Fire is a standard covered peril on most homeowners policies, and smoke damage and firefighting water are usually part of the same claim. Many policies also cover extra living expenses, like a hotel, while your home is repaired. Keep every receipt. Don't throw out damaged items until your adjuster has seen them or you have clear photos. Rebuilding after a fire is home improvement work, and Maryland requires those contractors to hold a Maryland Home Improvement Commission license. Electrical repairs need a licensed electrician.

## How to prevent smoke damage from spreading

!checks
- Keep the HVAC off until it's inspected.
- Close doors to rooms the smoke didn't reach.
- Lay down drop cloths in clean areas to avoid tracking soot.
- Don't use upholstered furniture until it's cleaned.
- Change HVAC filters after cleanup.
